# Neutrino Collision Term Integrator: Propagator vs. Approximation

## Overview
This Python script calculates and compares the energy density collision term for a $2 \rightarrow 2$ particle scattering process in a thermal bath. It evaluates the integral using two distinct theoretical approaches simultaneously:
1. **The Analytical Approximation (Eq. C33 style):** Treats the interaction as a point-like, four-fermion contact interaction.
2. **The Full Propagator:** Retains the exact momentum dependence of the mediator particle.

By using the `vegas` Monte Carlo integration library, the script calculates the exact percentage error introduced by the analytical approximation at varying temperatures and mediator masses.

---

## The Physics Context

### 1. The Analytical Approximation (Effective Field Theory)
In many cosmological Boltzmann solvers (like the one deriving Eq. C33), the collision term is simplified by assuming the mediator particle is much heavier than the energy of the colliding particles. 

If the momentum transfer $q$ is very small compared to the mediator mass $m$ ($q^2 \ll m^2$), the standard propagator mathematically collapses into a constant effective coupling:
$$\frac{g^2}{q^2 - m^2} \approx -\frac{g^2}{m^2}$$
This reduces the complex matrix element into a simple polynomial, allowing the phase-space integral to be solved analytically. 

### 2. The Full Propagator
In reality, the momentum transfer is not zero. For massless particles, the square of the momentum transfer is the Mandelstam variable $t$:
$$t = (p_1 - p_3)^2 = -2(p_1 \cdot p_3)$$
The exact matrix element squared $|\mathcal{M}|^2$ must include this term in the denominator:
$$|\mathcal{M}|^2 \propto \left(\frac{g^2}{t - m^2}\right)^2$$
Because $t$ is negative in scattering, this increases the denominator, thereby **suppressing** the total collision rate. 

---

## The Mathematical Approach

### Dimensional Reduction (12D to 5D)
A system of four interacting particles possesses 12 momentum dimensions (3 spatial dimensions per particle). However, solving a 12-dimensional Monte Carlo integral is practically impossible due to the Dirac delta function enforcing energy-momentum conservation.

This script elegantly reduces the phase space to **5 independent dimensions** by integrating out the overall spatial orientation (assuming an isotropic universe) and applying momentum conservation algebraically:
*   **$E_1, E_2$**: Initial energies of the colliding particles in the lab frame.
*   **$\cos\theta_{12}$**: The collision angle between the initial particles.
*   **$\cos\theta^*$**: The scattering angle of the outgoing particles in the Center-of-Mass (COM) frame.
*   **$\phi^*$**: The azimuthal scattering angle in the COM frame.

### Importance Sampling
To evaluate the integral efficiently, the uniform random variables generated by `vegas` (which span from 0 to 1) are mapped to an exponential distribution for $E_1$ and $E_2$. This ensures the algorithm spends its computational power resolving the peak of the thermal distribution rather than wasting time on ultra-high energies where the Fermi-Dirac statistics evaluate to zero.

## Interpreting the Results

### Why is there a large percentage difference?
If you run the script with $T = 10$ MeV and $m = 100$ MeV, you will see a roughly **50% difference** between the analytical approximation and the full calculation. 

**This is physically correct and expected.** 
*   In a thermal bath, the average particle energy is roughly $E \approx 3.15T$. 
*   For $T = 10$ MeV, collision energies easily reach 30 to 50 MeV in the thermal tails.
*   The momentum transfer $-t$ becomes a massive number, making the full denominator $(t - m^2)^2$ much larger than the approximated denominator $m^4$. 

The effective interaction (Eq. C33) heavily overestimates the collision rate at these temperatures. 

### The Regime of Validity
The analytical approximation is only valid when the bath temperature is much strictly lower than the mediator mass (typically $T < m/30$). 
*   **To test the approximation:** Set $T_1, T_2, T_3, T_4 = 1.0$ MeV (with $m = 100.0$ MeV). The difference will drop to less than 1%.
*   **Conclusion:** If your cosmological model involves temperatures that approach the mediator mass, you **must** use the full $q$-dependent propagator to accurately track $N_{\rm eff}$.
